See what your electricity costs, half-hour by half-hour

Open the half-hourly interval data from your Synergy account, for example MA_IntervalDataHistory.csv. The dashboard prices every half-hour on each of your tariffs and shows when you use the most.

Download your usage data from Synergy

  1. Log in to your Synergy account at my.synergy.net.au.
  2. Select the Usage tab at the top of the page.
  3. Check that you’re on the Interval Data page. Below the usage graph, select Export.
  4. Set Export as to CSV, choose half-hourly data intervals and the dates you want, then export. The file is saved to your computer, usually in your Downloads folder.
  5. Open the CSV here: drop it on this page, or use Choose files… below.

Synergy exports up to 90 days at a time. For a longer period, export each 90 days and open all the files together; where they overlap, each half-hour is only counted once. Later, add newer exports with Add CSV files… above the dashboard.

What the file needs to look like

A CSV with one row per half-hour and these columns (the order doesn’t matter):

  • Date: DD/MM/YYYY, MM/DD/YYYY or YYYY-MM-DD
  • Time: when the half-hour starts, like 13:30
  • PEAK (KWH), OFF PEAK (KWH), SUPER OFF PEAK (KWH): the reading goes in whichever column applies
  • Billing Status (optional): “Billed”, or anything else for readings not billed yet

Several files, or files joined into one by hand, are merged: each half-hour is counted once, preferring a billed reading, then the file whose readings run latest. Missing half-hours are listed so you know where the gaps are.

You can also drop a tariffs CSV (columns tariff, current, supply_cents_per_day, peak_cents_per_kwh, off_peak_cents_per_kwh, super_off_peak_cents_per_kwh) to replace the tariff list.

        About these figures
        • The readings come from the file you opened: one kWh reading per half-hour, labelled with the time the half-hour starts.
        • Each reading sits in one of three columns, which sets its rate period: off-peak 9 pm–9 am, super off-peak 9 am–3 pm, peak 3 pm–9 pm. The hours are the same every day, weekends included.
        • Tariff rates and supply charges come from the tariff list (Tariffs, at the top of the page), in cents. The tariff marked current there is the one the page opens on and compares against.
        • A half-hour’s energy cost is its kWh times its period’s rate. Each day also adds that tariff’s supply charge.
        • Every tariff is priced on the same half-hourly use. On a different tariff you might use electricity at different times.
        • The savings estimate moves the chosen share of every day’s peak kWh to the chosen period and prices it at that period’s rate. Total kWh stays the same.
        • The dates chosen at the top apply to every figure and chart. “Cost per day” compares the last 7 days up to the end of the chosen dates with the 7 days before them.
        • Unusual usage compares each day with up to 14 days either side of it. A day stands out when it used more than 9 in 10 of those days and at least 25% more than their middle value, or less than 9 in 10 of them and at least 25% less.
        • A spike is a half-hour that used at least 0.75 kWh more, and half as much again, as that time of day reaches on 9 in 10 of those days, so things that happen most days don’t count. Its difference is measured against the middle value for that time.
        • Always-on use is the average of each day’s four lowest half-hours, as watts. It counts as raised when it is at least 40% and 150 W above the middle value of the days around it for 3 days or more in a row. For a raised stretch, “What stood out” shows the always-on part of use: those days’ always-on watts kept up for 24 hours a day, in kWh, against the usual watts over the same hours, priced at the tariff’s rates weighted by the hours each one applies (as is the yearly always-on cost). Other use can rise or fall at the same time, so the line under it gives what all use on those days came to against usual.
        • Days with missing half-hours are left out of those comparisons, and “Data checks” lists them.
        • Daily kWh are rounded to 0.1 kWh and costs to the cent, so parts may not add up exactly.
        • “Not yet billed” readings have not appeared on a bill yet.
